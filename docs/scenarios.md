# Scenarios

Three buttons. Each one arms the next vehicle to pass Pod A and gives it a fate. Everything
else — traffic generation, vehicle speeds, timing, pod communication, the decision — runs
on its own.

Everything below happens inside the simulation: the traffic, the crash and the turn-off are
generated by the model, and the detector sees only pod events, never the scenario label.

The purpose of having three is to show that the detection logic is doing actual inference
from those events rather than reading the button you pressed.
**Scenarios B and C produce an identical signal at the moment of detection.** Everything
that separates them is gathered afterwards.

A note on how to read the screen: the top half is the road, which is ground truth — what
actually happened. The bottom half is the console, which shows only what ANTAR can
perceive. The detector is never told which button you pressed.

---

## Scenario A · Nominal

### What happens
A vehicle passes Pod A and drives the segment normally, sharing the road with the usual
mix of motorcycles, cars, autos, trucks and buses. It reaches Pod B.

### What the system observes
- A passage at Pod A: speed, length class, signature. An arrival window opens.
- A passage at Pod B inside that window, with a matching class and a signature distance
  well inside the 0.16 gate.

### What it concludes
Track closed. Normal pass. No candidate is raised, nothing is dispatched, and the
checklist ends after four steps.

### Why this scenario exists
It looks like the boring one, and it is the one most worth understanding.

Virtually every vehicle on the road produces this path. A detection system's usual state
is silence, and the value of a system like this is as much in how reliably it stays quiet
as in what it does when it fires. A system that raises candidates on ordinary traffic
gets switched off.

It also shows the machinery running underneath: while the tracked vehicle is in the
segment, the ACTIVE TRACKS table shows six or seven other windows open simultaneously,
each with its own countdown. The system is not following one vehicle at a time.

## Scenario B · Incident

### What happens
The tracked vehicle stops on the carriageway between the pods. A following vehicle runs
into it. The road is now blocked.

The simulated traffic behind reacts as the model says traffic does: hard braking with taillights
flaring, a slow crawl through the gap, vehicles swerving across the centre line to get
past, one stopping entirely with hazards on, and a queue building upstream. Smoke drifts
from the site.

### What the system observes
None of the above. The pods cannot see any of it. What they see is:

- The tracked vehicle's window expires with no matching arrival at Pod B.
- Over the following 90 seconds, arrivals at Pod B fall well short of what Pod A let
  through one travel-time earlier — in these runs, a shortfall of roughly 4–7 vehicles per
  minute.
- The vehicles that do reach Pod B are markedly slower than vehicles measured at Pod A.
- Three or four further windows expire unmatched in the same stretch.

### What it concludes
Confidence climbs out of the low tier, through medium, and clears the dispatch threshold.
Across repeated simulation runs it settles around **0.84** (0.83–0.89 in the 120-run
replay), and the evidence usually becomes overwhelming before the 90 seconds elapse, so the
early-dispatch threshold fires at around 30–40 seconds.

The alert dispatches to all three recipients. The transmission animates hop by hop: the
originating pod ripples outward, the wave reaches the next pod which lights and ripples
onward, the tower pulses with a wider wave at hand-off, and three separate paths reach the
hospital, the police and the local responders in sequence. The originating pod keeps a
slow steady pulse for as long as the incident stands.

### Why this scenario exists
This is the demonstration that corroboration works in the simulation.

The system did not detect a crash. It has no crash sensor and nothing told it a crash
occurred. It inferred that a stretch of road was blocked from the behaviour of vehicles
that were never involved in the incident — vehicles that arrived afterwards and simply
had to deal with what was in front of them.

The evidence for the crash was never on the vehicle that disappeared. It was on the ones
behind it.

## Scenario C · Legal exit

### What happens
The tracked vehicle indicates, slows, and turns off onto the village road at the junction
between the pods. It follows that road away into the dark and reaches the settlement at
the end of it. Nothing is wrong. This is a completely ordinary thing for a vehicle to do.

### What the system observes
- The tracked vehicle's window expires with no matching arrival at Pod B.

**This is exactly, bit for bit, the same observation as Scenario B.** A candidate opens,
identically.

Then, over the following 90 seconds:

- Arrivals at Pod B continue to match inflow at Pod A. Shortfall is zero.
- Speeds at Pod B hold at their normal level.
- No further windows go unmatched.

### What it concludes
Confidence stays in the low tier — around **0.29**, essentially the 0.18 the bare absence
is worth plus measurement noise. It never approaches the 0.66 dispatch threshold.

When the corroboration window closes, the candidate is **dismissed**. It is timestamped
and logged in full, including the numbers that produced the decision. No alert is sent to
anyone.

### Why this scenario exists
This is the one that decides whether the idea could ever be deployed.

Any system can fire an alert when something disappears. The difficulty is not firing when
something disappears for an ordinary reason — and on a real road, ordinary disappearances
would vastly outnumber crashes. A single turn-off on a monitored stretch would generate
unmatched windows all night long.

Run B and then C and watch the first thirty seconds of each. They are indistinguishable.
The system is not recognising a crash; it is asking the road a question and waiting for
the answer.

The refusal is the feature. Nothing was sent, and the reason nothing was sent is written
down.

## What to look for while demonstrating

**The checklist reads as an argument.** Each step carries a timestamp, the real numbers,
and a sentence explaining the reasoning. It should be possible to follow the system's
logic without narration.

**The flow readout is the evidence.** Expected against observed, with the shortfall
between them highlighted. In B the gap opens up and stays open. In C it stays flat. That
single comparison is what separates the two scenarios.

**Dismissed candidates are logged as prominently as dispatched alerts.** Scroll the event
log after Scenario C.

**The concurrent tracks table.** Six or seven windows open at once, each with its own
class, speed and countdown, in every scenario.

**The camera control.** WIDE is the default and never moves on its own — the presenting
view is stable. CLOSE pushes in on the incident site, which is useful for showing the
detail on the pod or the reaction of the traffic behind, but it is optional and manual.

## Reproducibility

Pressing a scenario button resets the road to a clean free-flow state and re-establishes
the statistical baseline before arming the next vehicle. This is deliberate: without it, a
Scenario C run immediately after a Scenario B run would inherit B's queue and depressed
flow figures, and would score against evidence that belonged to the previous incident.

The consequence you will see is that traffic visibly repopulates the moment you press a
button. That is the reset, not a glitch.

The scenarios are not scripted beyond the tracked vehicle's fate. Traffic is generated
randomly, so speeds, vehicle types and timings differ on every run, and the confidence
value at the end differs slightly each time. Across repeated runs the outcomes are stable:
A closes, B dispatches in the low-to-mid 0.8s, C is dismissed around 0.3.
