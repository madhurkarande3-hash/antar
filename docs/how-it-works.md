# How it works

This document explains the detection algorithm in detail: what each pod measures, how the
arrival window is computed, how a crash is told apart from an ordinary turn-off, and why
each of those decisions is made the way it is.

The numbers quoted here are the values actually used in `simulation/antar-sim.html`. The
logic is implemented and runs; the measurements it works from are generated by the traffic
model, not by a sensor on a road. Statements about how traffic behaves are assumptions the
model encodes, not findings from field data.

---

## 1. What a pod can actually see

This constrains everything else, so it is worth being precise about it.

A pod knows four things about a vehicle passing beneath it:

1. **That something passed**, and the timestamp.
2. **How fast it was going**, from the pod's speed measurement.
3. **Roughly how long it was**, which gives a coarse class: two-wheeler, car,
   three-wheeler, or heavy vehicle.
4. **A signature** — a short, repeatable physical fingerprint of the vehicle. It lets a
   second pod recognise "this is the same vehicle that passed the first pod" without
   knowing anything about whose vehicle it is. The detector is sensor-agnostic: it only
   needs four numbers that come out similar for the same vehicle at both pods. Candidate
   sources are a magnetic profile (inductive loop or magnetometer, a well-established
   traffic-engineering technique), a radar return, or the ultrasonic pair shown in the 3D
   pod concept. **The field sensor has not been selected yet**; the simulator generates
   the signature in software.

A pod does **not** know the registration number, the make or model, the occupants, the
destination, or the owner. The detector uses no camera (the 3D pod shows one only as an
optional expansion slot). The signature is a physical measurement, not
an identity, and it is meaningless outside the few minutes in which it is being matched.

Everything below is built from those four measurements and nothing else.

## 2. The arrival window

When a vehicle passes Pod A at time `t` with measured speed `v`, over a segment of length
`D` (700 m in the current model), the pod computes three timestamps:

```
expected arrival   tExp   = t + D / v
earliest plausible tEarly = t + D / (v × 1.30)
latest plausible   tLate  = t + max( 1.35 × D / v ,  D / 9.0 )
```

**The early edge** allows the vehicle to speed up by up to 30%. Beyond that it is not the
same journey.

**The late edge** is the interesting one, and it has two terms because one term is not
enough.

The first term, `1.35 × D / v`, allows the vehicle to take 35% longer than predicted —
ordinary variation.

The second term, `D / 9.0`, is a floor: the time it would take to cross the segment at
9 m/s (about 32 km/h) regardless of how fast the vehicle was going when it passed Pod A.

That floor exists because of a specific failure we hit while building the simulation. On a two-lane
undivided road, a car measured at 90 km/h can be stuck behind a tractor doing 35 km/h for
the rest of the segment. With a purely proportional window, that car's window expires
while the car is still on the road, perfectly fine, in a queue. The system then reports a
disappearance that never happened. On a road with mixed traffic — motorcycles, cars,
autos, trucks, buses all sharing one lane — the model produces this constantly, and real
mixed traffic would too.

The fix is to recognise that **no vehicle can cross the segment faster than the slowest
vehicle in front of it**. The late edge is therefore floored by the slowest thing
realistically on that road, not by the subject vehicle's own speed.

The practical effect: a fast car gets a window of about 45 seconds against an expected
28 seconds; a slow truck gets about 95 seconds. Both are generous, and both are still far
shorter than the time a genuinely stopped vehicle takes to not arrive, which is forever.

## 3. Matching at Pod B

When something passes Pod B, the system has to decide which open window — if any — it
closes. On a road with six or seven windows open at once, this is a real matching problem
and getting it wrong in either direction is costly.

A candidate window matches only if all three of these hold:

| Gate | Test |
|---|---|
| **Time** | The arrival falls between `tEarly` and `tLate`, with a 2-second grace either side |
| **Class** | The length class at Pod B equals the length class at Pod A |
| **Signature** | Euclidean distance between the two signature readings is below 0.16 |

Among windows that pass all three, the one with the closest signature match wins.

The signature gate is what makes concurrent tracking honest in the model. Without it, matching would be
guesswork: with several vehicles in the segment, an arrival could plausibly close any of
several open windows, and the system would end up attributing arrivals to the wrong
vehicles — which means it would also attribute *absences* to the wrong vehicles. The
signature is what lets a pod say "this specific vehicle arrived" rather than "a vehicle
arrived, roughly on time, probably that one".

The class gate is cheap and catches the obvious mismatches — a bus does not close a
motorcycle's window.

If no open window matches, the arrival is logged as an unmatched arrival. If a window
reaches `tLate` with nothing matching it, it is marked **missed**, and this is where the
interesting part begins.

## 4. An absence is not a crash

A missed window means one thing only: a vehicle entered the segment and did not leave it
past Pod B within the plausible time.

Things that produce exactly this signal:

- A crash.
- A turn onto a village road or a field track.
- Stopping at a roadside stall, a dhaba, a temple.
- Pulling onto the shoulder to take a call or check a tyre.
- A breakdown — which may or may not need help.

From Pod A these are indistinguishable. They are, in the information available, the same
event. This is the central difficulty of the whole approach, and any honest description of
the system has to start by admitting that the primary signal is ambiguous.

So a missed window opens a **candidate** — internally a *segment event* — and dispatches
nothing.

One detail worth noting: the event belongs to the **segment**, not to the vehicle.
Whichever absence is noticed first opens the event, and every later absence in that
segment folds into the same event rather than starting a competing one. This matters
because when a road is blocked, many vehicles go missing, and they do not go missing in
the order they arrived — a fast vehicle behind a slow one has a shorter window and expires
first. Treating these as one event about a stretch of road, rather than several events
about several vehicles, is both more accurate and easier to act on. An ambulance is sent
to a place, not to a vehicle.

## 5. Corroboration

For the next **90 seconds**, the system stops watching the vehicle that vanished and
starts watching the traffic behind it.

The reasoning:

> A vehicle blocking the carriageway changes what the vehicles behind it do.
> A vehicle that turned off changes nothing.

Three independent things are measured.

### 5.1 Downstream flow against upstream inflow

The primary evidence. If the road is blocked, fewer vehicles get through.

```
expected = vehicles counted at Pod A in the 90 s window ending one travel-time ago
observed = vehicles counted at Pod B in the last 90 s
shortfall = expected − observed          (veh/min)
ratio     = observed / expected
```

The lag matters. Comparing Pod A's count *now* against Pod B's count *now* would be
comparing different vehicles. The inflow is therefore offset by one nominal travel time,
estimated continuously from recent upstream speeds.

An earlier version of this compared each vehicle's individual predicted arrival time
against actual arrivals. It failed for the same reason the window floor was needed:
platooning. A car stuck behind a tractor arrives 30 seconds after its own prediction, and
with per-vehicle expectations that reads as a shortfall even though every vehicle
eventually arrives. Counting **rates over a window** instead of per-vehicle ETAs absorbs
that scatter — vehicles arriving late still get counted, just slightly later. The
comparison only breaks down when vehicles stop arriving at all, which is exactly the
condition worth detecting.

A ratio is only computed when at least 5 vehicles are expected in the window. Below that
the sample is too small to mean anything, and the system says so rather than guessing.

### 5.2 Downstream speed against upstream speed

Vehicles that get past a blockage are assumed to get past it slowly — braking, crawling
through the gap, picking their way past debris — and the model reproduces that. Mean speed at Pod B over the last 45 seconds is
compared against mean speed at Pod A over the last 120 seconds.

Pod A is always upstream of the incident, so it is a clean baseline that the incident
cannot contaminate. This is a small but important property: the reference is structurally
unaffected by the thing being measured.

### 5.3 Further unmatched windows

If the road is blocked, the vehicles queued behind the blockage also fail to arrive. Each
of those is another absence. One missing vehicle is ambiguous; four missing vehicles from
the same stretch within ninety seconds is not.

## 6. Confidence

The three measurements combine into a single continuous confidence value:

```
confidence = 0.18                                          (the absence itself)
           + up to 0.46   from the flow shortfall
           + up to 0.22   from the downstream speed drop
           + up to 0.20   from additional unmatched windows
```

Each term has a dead zone before it starts contributing, so ordinary noise does not
accumulate into a score:

| Term | Formula | Starts counting at |
|---|---|---|
| Flow | `clamp((1 − ratio − 0.10) / 0.55) × 0.46` | more than 10% shortfall |
| Speed | `clamp((drop − 0.08) / 0.40) × 0.22` | more than 8% slower |
| Knock-on | `clamp(extra / 3) × 0.20` | the second absence |

The weights say something about what the design trusts. Flow carries the most weight
because a sustained shortfall in vehicles reaching Pod B is judged the hardest signal to
produce by accident. That is a design judgement, tuned against the model rather than
established from road data. The bare absence is worth only
0.18 on its own, deliberately: it can never reach the dispatch threshold by itself, no
matter how long you wait.

### Tiers

| Tier | Range | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | below 0.35 | No dispatch. Candidate will be dismissed if it stays here. |
| **Medium** | 0.35 – 0.66 | Evidence accumulating, still no dispatch. |
| **High** | 0.66 and above | Dispatch threshold cleared. |

At 0.82 and above the detector dispatches immediately rather than waiting out the
remaining window. The reasoning: if flow has collapsed, speeds have dropped and three other
vehicles are missing, another forty seconds of confirmation adds little, and in a real
deployment those seconds would come out of the injured person's golden hour.

Otherwise the decision is taken when the 90 seconds elapse.

### The refusal case

If confidence has not cleared 0.66 when the window closes, the candidate is **dismissed**.
Not escalated, not sent as a low-priority notice, not held open in case something turns up
later. It is closed, timestamped, and logged with the numbers that produced the decision,
and the road goes back to being quiet.

This is a design position, not a limitation. A system that hedges — that sends a "possible
incident, confidence low" message — pushes the cost of its uncertainty onto a control room
that has to triage it, and eventually gets ignored entirely. The cost of a false alarm
should stay with the system.

Dismissed candidates are still recorded in full. If the parameters are wrong, the log is
where that becomes visible.

## 7. Why 90 seconds

Long enough for the traffic behind to arrive and react. At the roughly seven vehicles a
minute the model generates, ninety seconds is about ten vehicles, which is the sample the
flow comparison works from. It is a starting parameter chosen against the model, and real
road data would almost certainly move it.

Short enough that it is a small fraction of the golden hour. Ninety seconds of
verification to avoid sending an ambulance to a vehicle that turned off at a junction is a
trade worth making, and in clear-cut cases the early-dispatch threshold cuts it shorter
anyway.

The number is a starting point, not a result. It is one of the first things that should be
tuned against real road data.

## 8. Known weaknesses

Stated plainly, because they are the questions a careful reader will ask.

- **A crash that does not block the carriageway** produces an absence with no corroborating
  evidence — a vehicle that leaves the road cleanly and comes to rest out of everyone's
  way. The traffic behind never reacts. This is the case the current approach handles
  worst, and it is a real gap.
- **Very low traffic.** At 2 a.m. on a quiet road there may be no vehicles behind for
  several minutes. With nothing to corroborate against, confidence stays low. The
  sample-size floor stops the system from inventing evidence, but it also means the
  quietest roads — arguably the ones that need this most — are the hardest to serve. A
  longer corroboration window at low flow is the obvious direction.
- **Non-crash blockages.** A broken-down truck, a herd of animals, waterlogging, or a
  religious procession all produce exactly the crash signature. The system would dispatch.
  Whether that is a false positive or a useful road-condition alert depends on who is
  receiving it.
- **Sensor reality.** All of the above assumes speed, length and signature can be measured
  reliably in rain, heat and dust, on a pole that vibrates. That assumption is untested.

None of these are fatal, and several have obvious mitigations. They are not solved here.
